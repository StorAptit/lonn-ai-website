01 · Introduction

LLMs became infrastructure, and the bill became hard to predict.

Companies that use large language models through an API pay per token, with output tokens usually priced higher than input. New models arrive almost every month, and prices between them can differ by more than ten times, in extreme cases more than a hundred. A choice that made sense a few months ago can be expensive or outdated today.

Usage is changing too. Short one-off questions have given way to long conversations, multi-step workflows and agents that make many calls on their own, and reasoning models generate extra tokens before they answer. Companies are left unsure on two levels: what next month will cost, and whether they use the right model for each task. Usually all they have to go on is a single total bill from each provider.

The research question, in four parts

Technical

Difficulty estimation

How well can a prompt’s difficulty be estimated before it is answered?

Technical

Routing decision

How should that estimate be combined with prices, and the cost of reasoning, to pick a model?

Technical

Cost and quality

How large is the saving compared with always using a strong model, and what does it cost in quality?

Business

Adoption

What does it take to turn a prototype into a product ordinary companies can use?

The proposed solution

Lönn AI is an API middleware. A company replaces its provider’s key and endpoint with Lönn’s. For every request, Lönn identifies the kind of task, picks the model in a small, curated pool with the best balance of quality and cost for it, and forwards the request to the provider that serves that model. The choice is based on measured accuracy and billed cost per task type, not on list prices. One budget setting decides how much weight cost gets compared with quality.

Scope

The pre-study covers LLM use through APIs only, not subscriptions to chat assistants such as ChatGPT or Copilot. The prototype covers the core routing mechanism and is evaluated in a test setting, not on real customer traffic. Prompt compression, cost tracking and billing are outside the prototype, and data security is treated theoretically.

The benchmark

690 prompts across five task families: maths (MATH-500), programming (HumanEval+, MBPP+, LiveCodeBench), knowledge (MMLU-Pro, GPQA Diamond, ARC-Challenge), instruction following (IFEval) and business writing (WritingBench). Each of the eight models answered every prompt once, giving 5,520 outputs, each stored with its grade and billed cost, including reasoning tokens. Grading used reference answers, executable tests, rule-based checks or an LLM judge with the WritingBench rubric, depending on the task.

The routing rule

Prompts are grouped into 15 task types. A classifier (bge-small embeddings and a linear layer) estimates the probability p(t) of each type. For every task type and model, the observed pass rate and mean billed cost are known. Each model is scored as:

score(m) = Σt p(t) · accuracy(t, m) − λ · Σt p(t) · cost(t, m)

Because both terms are weighted by the task probabilities, an uncertain classification gives a smooth decision rather than a hard mistake. λ sets how much weight cost gets. The default is λ = 20.

Evaluation

Every strategy was evaluated by replaying its choices against the stored outputs, using five-fold cross-validation at prompt level, repeated over ten random partitions, so no test prompt ever informed its own routing table. The router was compared with the strongest fixed model and with an oracle that, in hindsight, picks the cheapest model that answered each prompt correctly.

03 · Assumptions and requirements

Three conditions for feasibility.

The service must create enough value to justify its fee, pick models reliably enough to make routing worthwhile, and handle data under conditions customers accept.

Business

Value that can be captured

A1 API costs are high enough that routing creates real savings and transparency.
A2 Part of that value can be captured with pricing customers accept.

R1–R3 Measurable value after fees; visibility and control over spend; a pricing and payment model that limits credit risk towards providers.

Technical

Predictable strengths

A3 Models have complementary strengths that are predictable enough for per-request selection to beat any single model.
A4 Routing adds little cost and latency.

R4–R5 Lower cost at comparable quality and latency; automatic fallback and modular updates as models and prices change.

Trust and safety

Data customers can hand over

A5 Standard data-handling settings and documented safeguards are enough assurance.
A6 Provider configurations can be verified well enough to enforce them.

R6–R7 Settings for processing location, retention and training use, including zero data retention; security controls, agreements and documented procedures.

On trust, Lönn matches each customer’s settings against verified provider endpoint conditions to decide which models are eligible for a request, and re-checks those conditions over time. ISO/IEC 27001 certification is optional, so the initial approach relies on documented controls, processing agreements and implementation evidence.

Positioning

Lönn sits between the broad model access of OpenRouter and the research-driven routing of Martian. The goal is not hundreds of possible routes but better decisions across a small, carefully chosen set of models and providers, acting as a dynamic optimisation layer that keeps adjusting as prices and models change.

05 · Stakeholders and interviews

Two people inside every customer, and three conversations that shaped the product.

Primary

Customers

Companies that pay for LLM tokens. The budget owner (CTO, head of engineering) decides and wants lower, predictable cost. Developers integrate and want a small change that doesn’t alter app behaviour.

Primary

LLM providers

Suppliers of every answer, and increasingly competitors as they build their own cost features. Working with several providers reduces dependence on any one.

Secondary

Regulators

GDPR and the EU AI Act shape what data can pass through the product and how it may be stored.

Secondary

Competitors and investors

Routing and gateway providers compete for the same customers. Investors will look for real savings, real customers and a product that is hard to copy.

06 · Results

The research says routing works. Our prototype agrees.

Evidence from the routing literature

  • Models succeed on different tasks. Across 21 LLMs and 16 benchmarks, an oracle matching the strongest fixed model’s score cut token costs by 85.2% on average (Fowler et al., 2026).
  • The differences are predictable. RouteLLM kept 95% of GPT-4’s MT-Bench score at an estimated 73% lower cost, with routing overhead of at most 0.4% (Ong et al., 2025).
  • Routing can beat the best model. Avengers-Pro matched GPT-5’s accuracy at 31.7% lower cost, or reached 4.0% higher accuracy in a quality-first setting (Li et al., 2026).
  • New models can be added. ZeroRouter routes to models outside its training pool, so the pool can be updated without retraining (Yan et al., 2026).

The Lönn Router prototype

The prototype has three parts: a classifier that identifies the likely task type, a router that ranks the eight models by expected quality and cost, and an executor that calls the chosen model and records the cost. If a call fails, the executor tries the next model. If the output is empty, cut off or fails a code example in the prompt, it makes one extra call.

07 · How we got there

Four iterations, four lessons.

1. The topic router

The first version classified prompts into four broad topics with Laya, an open ModernBERT-based classifier, and scored models with public Arena Elo and list prices. It reached 78.1% for $1.10, but sent 94% of prompts to one model, and knowing the true topic improved it by only 0.5 points.

Lesson: the routing logic, not the classifier, was the limit. Four topics were too broad, the complexity score barely varied, and list prices predicted real cost poorly.

2. Task types and a measured routing table

Switching to 15 task types and measured accuracy and cost matched the topic router’s quality at about a third of the cost (78.9% for $0.40), or gave six points more at a lower cost. A small bge-small classifier, at 34 ms per prompt, came within 0.1 points of using the true task type.

Lesson: once the logic was right, the classifier was no longer the bottleneck on the benchmark.

3. Everyday prompts

Real user prompts from the WildChat dataset look little like benchmark questions. Adding about 3,000 of them to the training data raised task-type accuracy on an everyday test set from 31% to 51%.

Lesson: benchmark results are optimistic for real traffic, and data from real use is what improves the classifier most.

4. Closing the gap to the oracle

Predicted complexity, agreement between two cheap models and a cheap writing judge were all tested and dropped. What worked was checking the answer: if it is empty, cut off or fails a code example in the prompt, the request is sent once more to the next-best model. Those answers would almost always be graded wrong, so the retry mostly turns failures into passes. At λ = 20 it raised accuracy from 83.7% to 85.8%, at a cost of $1.58 instead of $0.91, and at most of the budgets tested it beat spending the same money on routing alone.

Lesson: most of the oracle’s lead cannot be predicted from the prompt alone. Checking the answer afterwards recovers part of it.

09 · Roadmap

A plan of action, not a schedule.

  1. Find initial partnersAI-heavy companies such as Stockholm startups that rely on LLM APIs. Pilot partners pay only model costs, and share usage data and feedback in return.
  2. Run pilotsStart in shadow mode, where the router only records what it would have chosen, then gradually take over real traffic.
  3. Prepare for launchBuild the pilot data and insights into the product. Partners move to standard pricing as the first paying customers.
  4. Go liveAny company can create an account, get a Lönn API key and route its requests. Break-even is the first commercial milestone.
  5. Grow after launchUse partners as references and growing usage data to keep improving the router.

Technical next steps

  • Model onboarding without retraining, by learning model capability vectors from observed performance.
  • Evaluation on broader, representative data and repeatable tests, including latency.
  • Trust and data handling enforced across Lönn and downstream providers, with ongoing verification.
  • Reliable execution: provider integrations, fallback and monitoring under real workloads.
  • Metering and billing with current prices and prepaid balances to limit financial exposure.
  • Caching and session memory across model switches, and prompt and reasoning optimisation to cut tokens further.
10 · Limitations

What the results do and don’t show.

  • All results are measured on a 690-prompt benchmark, not real customer traffic. Savings and quality will depend on each application’s mix of tasks.
  • The classifier is less accurate on everyday prompts than on benchmark questions, which is why real usage data is the first goal of the pilots.
  • Data security, the dashboard, cost tracking and billing are designed but not implemented in the prototype.
  • The profitability model rests on scenario assumptions about pricing, usage and costs.
