01 · Introduction

Companies spend more on AI every year, and struggle to see what it buys.

Background

Organisations use AI both through ready-made applications such as ChatGPT and by building large language models into their own products and internal systems through an API. Enterprise spending on generative AI is growing fast, and a large part of it goes to those API integrations.

Enterprise generative AI

$37 billion

estimated spending in 2025, up from $11.5 billion in 2024 (Menlo Ventures, 2025)

Model APIs

$12.5 billion

of that went to foundation model APIs (Menlo Ventures, 2025)

Multi-model use

37%

of 100 surveyed CIOs used five or more models in 2025, up from 29% a year earlier (Andreessen Horowitz, 2025)

  • Billing is per token. Input and output tokens are priced separately, and output usually costs more. The total depends on how many requests are sent, how many tokens each uses and which models handle them.
  • Choosing a model is hard. Public benchmarks help, but a strong score does not guarantee a model suits a particular application. Companies weigh benchmarks against their own testing, cost, reliability, security and existing cloud relationships.
  • The market keeps moving. Providers keep releasing models with different capabilities and prices. Companies buy from several vendors because models differ by use case, yet prompts, guardrails and evaluations become tied to one model, so switching takes engineering and revalidation.
  • Spending is hard to trace. Providers report usage and cost, but not which business activities caused it. Linking spend to teams, features or processes across several providers takes extra integration and maintenance.

Problem

Every AI request has a cost, but not every request needs the same level of capability. Developers need reliable performance, while finance needs to understand and control spending. The problem has two parts.

Part 1

The cost-quality trade-off

An expensive model on a routine task wastes money. A cheaper model on a demanding task can compromise the result. How much is worth paying for the quality each task requires?

Part 2

Traceability

The total bill does not show which features, workflows or customer activities generated it, or whether the quality delivered justified the cost.

Solution

Lönn AI sits between a company’s applications and its AI providers as a single API layer. It handles model selection and makes the resulting spending visible.

  • Routing. Each task goes to a suitable model, based on the quality it requires, the cost of serving it and the company’s security and data-processing requirements. The choices stay aligned as models and prices change, without developers having to monitor the market by hand.
  • Cost tracking. The same layer connects spending to the features, workflows and customer activities behind it. Developers see what their applications cost to run, and finance sees what drives expenditure.

Scope

The pre-study covers organisations that integrate hosted LLMs into their own products and workflows through token-billed APIs, not subscriptions to ready-made chat assistants such as ChatGPT or Microsoft Copilot. It evaluates technical, commercial and regulatory feasibility along two tracks. The intended product and the prototype are not identical.

Track 1 · Theoretical

The intended product

Defines and evaluates the full product from academic literature, provider documentation, participatory design interviews and financial modelling: the routing architecture, expenditure attribution and customer controls, data governance and the commercial model.

Track 2 · Empirical

The prototype

Tests whether automated model selection cuts cost while preserving answer quality. Every tested prompt needs paid API calls, so it is limited to the core routing mechanism, evaluated offline on a fixed benchmark with a limited pool of models.

Cost tracking, billing, data-security enforcement and prompt compression are not implemented in the prototype. They are covered conceptually in the product design and in the roadmap.

02 · Method

An iterative feasibility study, with one method per assumption.

The team listed the technical, business and trust assumptions the service depends on and chose a way to test each. Findings from one activity fed the next: stakeholder feedback shaped which routing decisions customers control, prototype comparisons guided the routing logic, and the measured cost difference fed the financial model.

Literature & market

Routing research and competitors

Do models succeed on different requests, can a router predict which one to use, and can new models be added without retraining? Competitors compared by customers, routing, controls and pricing. Provider documentation reviewed for processing location, retention and training use.

Participatory design

A founding engineer at a YC-backed startup, an operations specialist in sensitive data handling, and a potential customer on expected token volumes.

Prototype

The benchmark

690 prompts across five task families: maths (MATH-500), programming (HumanEval+, MBPP+, LiveCodeBench), knowledge (MMLU-Pro, GPQA Diamond, ARC-Challenge), instruction following (IFEval) and business writing (WritingBench). Each of the eight models answered every prompt once, giving 5,520 outputs, each stored with its grade and billed cost, including reasoning tokens. Grading used reference answers, executable tests, rule-based checks or an LLM judge with the WritingBench rubric, depending on the task.

The routing rule

Prompts are grouped into 15 task types. A classifier (bge-small embeddings and a linear layer) estimates the probability p(t) of each type. For every task type and model, the observed pass rate and mean billed cost are known. Each model is scored as:

score(m) = Σt p(t) · accuracy(t, m) − λ · Σt p(t) · cost(t, m)

Because both terms are weighted by the task probabilities, an uncertain classification gives a smooth decision rather than a hard mistake. λ sets how much weight cost gets. The default is λ = 20.

Evaluation

Every strategy was evaluated by replaying its choices against the stored outputs, using five-fold cross-validation at prompt level, repeated over ten random partitions, so no test prompt ever informed its own routing table. The router was compared with the strongest fixed model and with an oracle that, in hindsight, picks the cheapest model that answered each prompt correctly.

03 · Assumptions and requirements

Three conditions for feasibility.

The service must create enough value to justify its fee, pick models reliably enough to make routing worthwhile, and handle data under conditions customers accept.

Business

Value that can be captured

A1 API costs are high enough that routing creates real savings and transparency.
A2 Part of that value can be captured with pricing customers accept.

R1–R3 Measurable value after fees; visibility and control over spend, with usage breakdowns, forecasts and routing preferences; a pricing and payment model that captures a profitable share of the savings and limits credit risk towards providers.

Technical

Predictable strengths

A3 Models have complementary strengths that are predictable enough for per-request selection to give a better cost-quality trade-off than one fixed model, and they can be re-estimated as models enter or leave the pool.
A4 Routing adds little cost and latency.

R4–R5 Lower cost at comparable quality and acceptable latency, with minimal integration effort; automatic fallback and modular updates as models and prices change.

Trust and safety

Data customers can hand over

A5 Standard data-handling settings and documented safeguards are enough assurance.
A6 Provider configurations can be verified well enough to enforce them.

R6–R7 Settings for processing location, retention and training use, including zero data retention; security controls, agreements and documented procedures.

On trust, Lönn matches each customer’s settings against verified provider endpoint conditions to decide which models are eligible for a request, and re-checks those conditions periodically and after relevant changes. ISO/IEC 27001 certification is optional, so the initial approach relies on documented controls, processing agreements and implementation evidence. Whether customers accept this level of assurance is still an assumption to validate.

Positioning

Lönn should be positioned between the broad model access of platforms such as OpenRouter and the more intelligence-driven routing of companies such as Martian. The aim is not to compete on the number of models and providers, but to make better decisions across a diverse and complementary set of them, with cost-efficient inference that keeps output quality. As a dynamic optimisation layer that keeps adjusting as prices and models change, it can also give customers more stable quality and more predictable spending.

05 · Stakeholders and interviews

Five stakeholder groups, and three conversations that shaped the product.

Customers

Two roles in one company

Companies that use LLM provider APIs and pay for the tokens. Leadership or the budget owner (a CTO or head of engineering) decides and wants lower, more predictable cost with maintained quality. Developers integrate and want a small change that doesn’t alter how the app behaves.

LLM providers

Suppliers of every request

Without their models there is no product. A price change, a limit on access or a competing feature from a large provider can change the conditions quickly. Using several providers reduces this dependence.

Regulators

GDPR and the EU AI Act

They shape what data can pass through the product and how it can be stored. Where data is processed and whether it is stored affects both the design and customer trust.

Competitors

Routing and gateway providers

They compete directly for the same customers, including large cloud providers that build routing into their own platforms.

Investors

Not yet engaged

Not involved at the pre-study stage, but they matter for whether the venture can grow beyond a prototype.

What the interviews told us

Product development

A founding engineer at Zaplar

Zaplar builds an agentic hotel system on LLM APIs. Routing fits best where one agent handles a mix of easy and hard tasks. But some features need fixed quality, so customers need a way to balance quality and price. That became λ.

Trust

An operations specialist

From an AI startup handling sensitive health data: work towards ISO certification, GDPR compliance, 30-day retention, EU hosting and encryption at rest. Also helped with the EU AI Act and a risk-assessment approach based on probability and impact.

Customer insight

A potential customer

A B2B product with about ten business users would need roughly 100 million to 1 billion tokens per month. This sized the customer in the financial model.

06 · Results

The research says routing works. Our prototype agrees.

Evidence from the routing literature

  • Models succeed on different tasks. Across 21 LLMs and 16 benchmarks, an oracle that always picks the right model scored 88% on average, against 82.4% for the strongest fixed model. When it only had to match that model’s score, it cut token costs by 85.2% on average (Fowler et al., 2026).
  • The differences are predictable. RouteLLM kept 95% of GPT-4’s MT-Bench score at an estimated 73% lower cost, with routing overhead of at most 0.4% (Ong et al., 2025).
  • Routing can beat the best model. Avengers-Pro matched GPT-5’s accuracy at 31.7% lower cost, or reached 4.0% higher accuracy in a quality-first setting (Li et al., 2026).
  • New models can be added. ZeroRouter routes to models outside its training pool, so the pool can be updated without retraining (Yan et al., 2026).

The proposed product

Customer controls

API, SDK and web app

A dashboard with forecasts, usage by team, task and model, and savings after fees. Saved data policies and a cost-quality setting per call.

Request routing

Eligible, cheapest, good enough

The chosen data policy and verified endpoint conditions define which models are eligible. Fallback stays within the same limits. Prepaid balances limit credit exposure.

Evaluation and improvement

Learning from traffic

Where customers permit it, retained prompts support clustering and analysis of routing behaviour, with separate validation of answer quality.

The Lönn Router prototype

The prototype has three parts: a classifier that identifies the likely task type, a router that ranks the eight models by expected quality and cost, and an executor that calls the chosen model and records the cost. If a call fails, the executor tries the next model. If the output is empty, cut off or fails a code example in the prompt, it makes one extra call.

07 · How we got there

The earlier iterations, and what each one taught us.

1. The topic router

The first version classified prompts into four broad topics with Laya, an open ModernBERT-based classifier, and scored models with public Arena Elo and list prices. It reached 78.1% for $1.10, but sent 94% of prompts to one model, and knowing the true topic improved it by only 0.5 points.

Lesson: the routing logic, not the classifier, was the limit. Four topics were too broad, the complexity score barely varied, and list prices predicted real cost poorly.

2. Task types and a measured routing table

Switching to 15 task types and measured accuracy and cost matched the topic router’s quality at about a third of the cost (78.9% for $0.40), or gave six points more at a lower cost. A small bge-small classifier, at 34 ms per prompt, came within 0.1 points of using the true task type.

Lesson: once the logic was right, the classifier was no longer the bottleneck on the benchmark.

3. Everyday prompts

Real user prompts from the WildChat dataset look little like benchmark questions. Adding about 3,000 of them to the training data raised task-type accuracy on an everyday test set from 31% to 51%.

Lesson: benchmark results are optimistic for real traffic, and data from real use is what improves the classifier most.

4. Closing the gap to the oracle

With task types the router reached 84% against the oracle’s 94%. Two task types, algorithmic programming and business writing, account for 68% of that gap. Predicted complexity, agreement between two cheap models and a cheap writing judge were all tested and dropped. What worked was checking the answer: if it is empty, cut off or fails a code example in the prompt, the request is sent once more to the next-best model. Those answers would almost always be graded wrong, so the retry mostly turns failures into passes. At λ = 20 it raised accuracy from 83.7% to 85.8%, at a cost of $1.58 instead of $0.91, and at most of the budgets tested it beat spending the same money on routing alone.

Lesson: most of the oracle’s lead cannot be predicted from the prompt alone. Checking the answer afterwards recovers part of it.

09 · Discussion

Did the pre-study meet its own requirements?

The discussion goes back to the requirements set at the start and weighs the evidence for each group.

Business · R1–R3

Partially met

On the benchmark the router gives clear financial value: 63.3% lower AI spend after the fee at λ = 20. The 20% fee with prepaid credits reaches profitability at about two typical customers and removes most credit risk. λ gives control, and the dashboard is designed to give visibility.

Why only partially: the evidence is a benchmark and single interviews. No customer has yet been asked whether they would pay the fee, and savings depend on each customer’s mix of tasks.

Technical · R4–R5

Met on the benchmark

About 97% of Sonnet’s accuracy at about a fifth of its cost at λ = 20, and higher accuracy at about half the cost at λ = 10. The classifier takes 34 ms, so the router’s own overhead is negligible. The prototype falls back when a provider call fails, and the routing table can be rebuilt when prices change.

Reservations: only 690 prompts with one stored answer per model, and exam-style questions rather than real customer prompts. A new model must run the full benchmark, and fallback is untested under realistic load.

Trust and safety · R6–R7

Planned, not yet built

The data-handling settings, security controls and documentation were discussed and planned with an operations specialist at an AI startup that handles sensitive health data, but not developed in practice. Building them is a roadmap item.

Priorities named: EU hosting, GDPR compliance, encryption, a 30-day retention limit and working towards ISO certification.

Two risks the discussion raises

  • Supplier risk. With Lönn the provider becomes interchangeable, and the customer may not know which model answered. A provider that sees this as lost brand recognition or customer relationships can raise prices or restrict access, as when Anthropic cut Windsurf’s access to Claude in 2025 (Zeff, 2025). A pool spread across several providers reduces the risk, but losing the strongest models would lower the value of the whole product.
  • Security against savings. Stricter data-processing requirements leave fewer eligible providers, and several of the cheaper models may be excluded. Customers with very strict requirements may save much less than the benchmark suggests.

Even with these reservations, the gap to always using Sonnet is large across the whole range of λ, which the report takes as a strong signal that the technical requirements can be met in practice.

10 · Roadmap

A plan of action, not a schedule.

  1. Find initial partnersA small number of companies that rely on LLM APIs in their products, for example AI-heavy startups in Stockholm such as Legora or Lovable, or growing startups such as Zaplar. Pilot partners pay only model costs. In return they share usage data on agreed terms, give feedback and let Lönn name them as partners.
  2. Run pilotsStart in shadow mode, where the router only records what it would have chosen, then gradually take over real traffic.
  3. Prepare for launchBuild the pilot data and insights into the product. Partners move to standard pricing as the first paying customers.
  4. Go liveAny company can create an account, get a Lönn API key and route its requests. Break-even is the first commercial milestone.
  5. Grow after launchUse partners as references and growing usage data to keep improving the router.

Technical next steps

  • Model routing: onboard new models without retraining the whole system, with capability vectors learned from observed performance.
  • Evaluation: broader, representative data and repeatable tests of quality, total cost and latency.
  • Trust and data handling: customer policies enforced across Lönn and downstream providers, with ongoing verification.
  • Reliable execution: provider integrations, fallback and monitoring tested under realistic workloads.
  • Customer controls and integration: the API, Python SDK and web app, connecting each request to its cost-quality preference and data policy.
  • Metering and billing: accurate usage tracking, current prices, and prepaid balances or credit limits.
  • Caching and session memory: how model switching affects caching and conversation context, and what that costs.
  • Prompt and reasoning optimisation: prompt compression and reasoning limits to cut tokens, validated against answer quality.
